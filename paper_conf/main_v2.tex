\begin{abstract}
The usual test of which answer a transformer prefers after contradictory training is pairwise: the correct completion against one incorrect one. When the error can take several forms, a frequency-matching model wins because the false side is divided among its variants. We measure the share of probability mass the correct completion holds against \emph{everything the error generator can produce}, which a frequency-matching model scores at exactly $0.500$. At 86M parameters the correct completion holds $59.0\% \pm 0.8\%$ of the mass against random errors (4 seeds, every per-seed bootstrap interval above $0.5$; $59.4\%$ on an independent corpus), whereas the single realized error gives $77.6\%$, a threefold overstatement of the excess above chance. Below 86M the share shows no preference on the primary corpus. Against the primary corpus's single coherent false rule, whose support is one string, the correct completion holds $43.5$--$48.8\%$ at every size; the shortfall below $0.5$ is completion length. Two experiments built to identify a compression mechanism raise the pairwise measures while the family share stays flat or falls; we report them as negative controls. A third varies only whether the model can learn which member of an enumerated false family a problem receives: the preference swings from $+5$ points for the correct system (unpredictable selector) to $-7$ (learnable selector). The objective favors a learnable, internally consistent system, not a true one; the contrast holds from 418M to 3.08B.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

Training corpora are noisy: the same question receives contradictory answers in different documents, and yet language models trained on such data tend to prefer the correct one. Without an account of \emph{why} this filtering works we cannot predict \emph{when} it fails, for instance on internally consistent errors.

Scaling \citep{kadavath2022language}, frequency \citep{kandpal2023large} and truth-correlated representations \citep{marks2024geometry} each predict factual accuracy, and calibrated models must hallucinate at a rate set by the corpus \citep{kalai2024calibrated}, but none of this says why the training objective alone would favor one answer over another at equal frequency and format. Minimizing cross-entropy minimizes code length \citep{shannon1948mathematical,deletang2024language}, which links training to the Minimum Description Length principle \citep{rissanen1978modeling,grunwald2007minimum} and suggests that an error the model cannot predict from the problem is harder to accommodate than one it can, so a \emph{coherent} false system would go unfiltered. This framing is motivation only: both of our attempts to establish it as the operative mechanism turn out to measure something else.

The pairwise measure used throughout the literature on contradictory data is not neutral: if the error can take $N$ forms, a model that reproduces training frequencies assigns $0.5$ to the correct string and $0.5/N$ to each false one and wins the comparison for every $N \geq 2$, so any experiment that raises $N$ raises pair accuracy for free. We remove this channel by summing over the whole false support,
\[
\text{share} \;=\; \frac{P(\text{correct})}{P(\text{correct}) + \sum_i P(\text{false}_i)}.
\]
On the primary denoising corpora each problem occurs exactly twice, once correct and once corrupted, and every other string in the enumeration occurs zero times, so a frequency-matching model scores exactly $0.500$ regardless of $N$.

We train transformers of 3.5M--3.08B parameters from scratch on mathematical corpora where each problem appears with correct and incorrect solutions, vary the structure of the errors at fixed frequency, format and domain, and compute both measures on the same checkpoints. Our contributions are as follows. (a)~A measurement of correctness preference that mass splitting cannot inflate, with an exact null, showing $59.0\%$ at 86M against random errors and no more than chance at 3.5M--26M on the primary corpus. (b)~Confirmation that a single coherent false rule, in every coherent condition we built, produces no preference at any size, immune to splitting because its false support is one string, with its apparent sub-chance readings traced to completion length. (c)~Two negative controls, a multi-rule sweep and a description-length ladder, in which pair accuracy (multi-rule) and the realized-support share (ladder) rise by 32 and 16 points while the family share stays flat or falls. (d)~A calibration showing that the pairwise measures overstate the excess above chance three- to fourfold in the one condition where all of them can be computed exactly. (e)~A learnable-selector control that varies the predictability of the selector over enumerated false families of the same order (ten strings against a mean of $17.5$), moving the preference from $+5$ to $-7$ points and reproducing at 1.12B--3.08B. Results elsewhere in the paper that rest on pair accuracy alone are marked as such.

\section{Related Work}
%======================================================================

\textbf{Prediction as compression.}\quad The link between prediction and compression traces to \citet{shannon1948mathematical} and \citet{solomonoff1964formal} and to Rissanen's MDL principle \citep{rissanen1978modeling,grunwald2007minimum}. \citet{deletang2024language} showed that language models are strong general-purpose compressors, \citet{huang2024compression} found a linear relation between compression quality and benchmark performance, and \citet{wan2025large} argue that LLM training approximates Solomonoff induction. \citet{pan2025understanding} link compression to knowledge acquisition, \citet{chlon2025predictable} link compression failures to hallucination, and contemporaneous preprints formalize simplicity bias through MDL \citep{marty2026compression} and connect compression to mathematical modeling \citep{aksenov2026compression}. This framing motivates our design.

\textbf{Representations, data statistics and truthfulness.}\quad Structured internal representations arise from prediction \citep{li2023a_emergent,gurnee2024language}, and several studies locate truth-correlated directions \citep{burns2023discovering,marks2024geometry,li2023b_inference,azaria2023internal,burger2024truth,ravfogel2025emergence}; we work at the behavioral level. Truthfulness tracks pretraining ``personas'' \citep{joshi2024personas} and document frequency \citep{elazar2022measuring,kandpal2023large}, and under conflicting knowledge the model follows formality and surface quality \citep{li2024formality}, contextual evidence \citep{longpre2021entity} or adaptive conflict resolution \citep{xie2024adaptive}. TruthfulQA \citep{lin2022truthfulqa} shows that models reproduce falsehoods common among humans, consistent with an objective that carries no prior toward truth; our coherent condition is the controlled form of that observation. We fix frequency, format and surface quality and vary only the structure of the error system.

\textbf{Simplicity bias, noisy labels and poisoning.}\quad Neural networks prefer simple functions \citep{valle2019deep,mingard2021sgd,goldblum2024no}, memorize random labels \citep{zhang2017understanding,arpit2017closer} yet tolerate massive label noise \citep{rolnick2017deep}, and grokking connects compression to generalization \citep{nanda2023progress,liu2023grokking}. The poisoning literature, from BadNets \citep{gu2017badnets} through concealed NLP poisoning \citep{wallace2021concealed} to web-scale poisoning \citep{carlini2024poisoning} and near-constant poison counts for LLMs \citep{souly2025poisoning}, studies coordinated, internally consistent corruption, which is the kind of corruption our coherent condition models; in that controlled setting we find no filtering of it by the objective.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
%======================================================================

\textbf{Hypothesis and MDL framing.}\quad Consider a corpus in which each problem appears with a correct answer (theory $T_1$) and an alternative ($T_2$). An MDL learner \citep{rissanen1978modeling,grunwald2007minimum} minimizing $L(M) + L(D|M)$ must encode \emph{both} clusters as a latent-source mixture, so when $K(T_2) \gg K(T_1)$ (random errors) the false cluster costs a memorized answer per problem, and when $K(T_2) \approx K(T_1)$ (coherent errors) there is no basis to prefer either (Appendix~\ref{app:mdl}). This motivated the design, and its graded consequence fails: a wrong rule storing more constants should be filtered more strongly, and Section~\ref{sec:ladder} varies exactly that and finds the share flat. We keep MDL as motivation and interpretation, not as a quantity these experiments measure; a gzip proxy for description length (Appendix~\ref{app:compression}) tracks pair accuracy only through the splitting-inflated multi-rule points. Throughout, ``truth'' means correctness of mathematical derivations and factual accuracy of Wikipedia paragraphs; models compress text, not reality.

\subsection{Models and Training}
\label{sec:models}

We train decoder-only transformers with a GPT-2-like design \citep{radford2019language} (pre-norm, GELU, causal mask) at four sizes: \emph{tiny} (4 layers, $d_{\text{model}}{=}256$, 4 heads, 3.5M parameters), \emph{small} ($6 \times 384$, 6 heads, 12M), \emph{medium} ($8 \times 512$, 8 heads, 26M) and \emph{large} ($12 \times 768$, 12 heads, 86M; Table~\ref{tab:models}, Appendix~\ref{app:reproducibility}). Section~\ref{sec:qwen} and Table~\ref{tab:learnable} extend to the Qwen3 architecture \citep{yang2025qwen3} at 418M--3.08B. Training uses AdamW (weight decay $0.01$), cosine decay with linear warmup, $\text{lr}=3\text{e-}4$, sequence length 256, batch size 32 and 5000 steps; learning curves plateau by step 3000--4000 at all sizes (Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}). Core conditions use 4 random seeds, and the seed count is stated with every table. The original denoising experiments use PyTorch \citep{paszke2019pytorch}, the multi-rule and Wikipedia experiments MLX~0.31.0 \citep{mlx2023}; the per-experiment hardware and software record is in Appendix~\ref{app:reproducibility}. Problems are generated and verified with SymPy~1.14 \citep{meurer2017sympy}; no human annotation is involved, and the generation templates are in the released repository.

\subsection{Corpus Design}
\label{sec:corpus}

\textbf{Denoising setup (primary).}\quad A generator writes mathematical problems of four types (multi-step arithmetic, factoring, equation solving, differentiation) as step-by-step derivations in English, and \textbf{each problem appears with contradictory answers}: once correct and once with an error. Each corpus contains 5{,}000 unique problems. The \emph{equal random} condition pairs each correct derivation with one random error (1:1), the \emph{equal coherent} condition with one error produced by a single systematic false rule applied consistently to every problem of a type (1:1), and the 1:2 and 1:4 conditions add two or four random errors per problem (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}; Table~\ref{tab:conditions}, Appendix~\ref{app:reproducibility}). The tokenizer is character-level (vocabulary ${\sim}57$); BPE robustness is checked in Section~\ref{sec:robustness}.

\textbf{Wikipedia setup (transfer).}\quad We construct corpora from 20{,}000 English-Wikipedia paragraphs sampled from the 2023-11-01 Wikimedia dump \citep{wikimedia2023enwiki}, with spaCy~3.8 (\texttt{en\_core\_web\_sm}) for named-entity recognition \citep{honnibal2020spacy}, in two corruption modes: \emph{random substitution} replaces each entity with a random entity of the same type, and \emph{coherent substitution} applies one consistent global mapping (every ``France'' $\to$ ``Japan'', every ``Paris'' $\to$ ``Tokyo''). Both preserve type and grammatical structure; random substitution breaks co-occurrence patterns. Models train on 50/50 mixes and are evaluated on 2{,}000 held-out paired paragraphs.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\textbf{Aggregate share (primary).}\quad For each problem we enumerate the full set of completions the error generator can produce, score each with the summed log-probability of its tokens conditioned on the shared prompt (true $\log P(\text{string} \mid \text{prompt})$, not a length-normalized score), and report the share defined in the introduction. The enumeration is exact: each generator has a finite error family, and the enumeration script drives the generator's own rendering functions and verifies for every problem that the error the corpus contains is a member of the enumerated set. Because each problem occurs exactly twice in the primary corpora and every other member of the family occurs zero times, a model that reproduces training frequencies scores exactly $0.500$ for any family size, and adding strings to the sum can only lower the share, so a value above $0.5$ is a lower bound on the preference. The share is also a full-string log-probability conditioned on the shared prompt rather than a per-token mean, so local token predictability inside a completion cannot inflate it through a per-token average, and the length-matched subset below removes the one remaining channel distinct from the preference itself, completion length.

\textbf{Statistics.}\quad Per-run 95\% confidence intervals are bootstrap intervals over problems (5000 resamples); the $\pm$ attached to a mean across seeds is the population standard deviation across seeds; pair-accuracy evaluations also store a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank $p$ on per-pair NLL differences and a bootstrap interval, released with the per-run outputs.

\textbf{Pair accuracy (secondary).}\quad Pair accuracy is the fraction of pairs where the model assigns lower mean NLL to the correct completion than to one sampled incorrect completion, equivalent to the Common Language Effect Size \citep{mcgraw1992common}. Reported for comparability with the prior literature, it is not a controlled diagnostic of preference: whenever the false side has more than one form, a frequency-matching model wins it without preferring anything (Section~\ref{sec:central}).

\textbf{Length control.}\quad Every measurement records the mean difference in completion length between the false and correct sides and the share restricted to problems where all completions have identical token length. Shorter strings carry more probability by construction (Section~\ref{sec:central}).

\textbf{Generative evaluation.}\quad We decode greedily on 500 test prompts and verify each answer with SymPy (Section~\ref{sec:generative}).

The aggregate share puts the correct completion at $59.0\% \pm 0.8\%$ of the probability mass at 86M parameters against everything the error generator can produce (Table~\ref{tab:central}), with all four per-seed bootstrap intervals above $0.5$: $[58.7, 60.4]$, $[57.5, 59.3]$, $[57.2, 59.0]$, $[59.0, 60.7]$. Because adding strings to the denominator can only lower the value, $59.0$ is a lower bound. An independent corpus gives a matching value at that size: on the standard (non-denoising) corpus, with a different generator and a mean of $5.51$ false modes per problem, the share is $59.4\% \pm 0.6\%$ (Appendix~\ref{app:standard}).

Below 86M the aggregate share shows no preference: $18.6\%$, $31.6\%$ and $44.1\%$ at 3.5M, 12M and 26M, all below the null, while pair accuracy at those sizes already reads $72$--$89\%$. Part of that shortfall is a downward bias of the measure at small scale rather than a preference for the false system. For models trained on the ladder corpus of Section~\ref{sec:ladder} with no false rule anywhere, the family share on its 18-string arithmetic support reads $99.7\%$ at 86M but only $64.1\%$ at 3.5M: a third of the mass at the smallest size leaks onto strings that never occurred. We have no clean calibration on the denoising support itself and take the ladder figure as the best available estimate of the leak.

The gap between the measures is what the pairwise comparison adds on its own. At 86M, scoring the correct completion against the single realized error gives $27.6$~pp of excess above chance where summing the family gives $9.0$~pp, a threefold overstatement, and pair accuracy against that same error gives $37.4$~pp, a fourfold one. Below 86M the measures disagree about whether there is an effect at all.

Against a coherent false rule no preference appears at any size: $43.5$, $45.8$, $46.4$ and $48.8\%$. One rule produces one string, so there is nothing to split, and this half of the contrast is immune to the objection by construction. The readings sit slightly below $0.5$ because the coherent false completions are on average $0.58$ tokens shorter than the correct ones; on the length-matched subset the values are $46.7$, $48.3$, $48.1$ and $49.3\%$, approaching chance from below rather than sitting under it. An earlier draft attributed the sub-chance readings to the textual simplicity of the false rules; that reading was wrong, and we draw no conclusion from the shortfall.

The first version of this work made this experiment its central identification argument: models train on corpora with $N$ compact wrong rules per task type, each problem's rule drawn at random, so that only the predictability of the problem-to-rule mapping changes, and pair accuracy jumps from $47\%$ at $N{=}1$ to $79\%$ at $N{=}2$. That jump is mass splitting. Aggregate share is $47.9\% \pm 0.7\%$ at $N{=}1$ and $47.8\% \pm 0.4\%$ at $N{=}2$ (Table~\ref{tab:multirule}; Figure~\ref{fig:multirule}, Appendix~\ref{app:controls}): the transition that carries 32 points of pair accuracy moves the allocation between the two answer systems by $0.1$ points. We had argued that splitting could not explain the jump because complete splitting would drive $N{=}2$ accuracy toward $100\%$; partial separation predicts what is observed, and the direct measurement supports that reading. The experiment is withdrawn as evidence for a selector cost and reported as a calibration of how much pair-accuracy signal splitting alone can manufacture. Beyond $N{=}2$ the decline is partly a property of the measure, which collects more diffuse uncertainty as more neighboring strings are summed; the safe reading is the absence of an increase.

The multi-rule design confounds description length with mode count. A cleaner test varies the description length of a \emph{single deterministic} false rule, $a \cdot b + T[\,|a| \bmod L\,]$ with $T$ a fixed table of $L$ offsets, so that $L$ is the number of constants the rule stores while the computation, the corruption load, the error magnitudes and the task distribution stay fixed and completion lengths differ by at most $0.044$ tokens. If preference were graded in description length, it should rise with $L$. Over the realized support it does, at every size. Summing the full family removes the rise and reverses it below 86M (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}, Figure~\ref{fig:central} right); at 86M the family reads $55.6$, $51.9$, $52.6$, $51.3$. The realized rise has an explanation that owes nothing to description length: a model that has not learned the table spreads its false mass over the entries and is credited for the spread.

\textbf{Calibration.}\quad The same four sizes trained on a corpus with the same problems and \emph{no false rule anywhere} (2 seeds) give an 18-string family share of $99.7\%$ at 86M, $97.3\%$ at 26M, $93.2\%$ at 12M and $64.1\%$ at 3.5M (Table~\ref{tab:clean_control}, Appendix~\ref{app:controls}). At the top size the measure is close to unbiased, so the flat ladder there is a real absence of grading; at the bottom the leak of Section~\ref{sec:central} makes small-model family numbers conservative rather than evidence about preference. With Section~\ref{sec:multirule}, both experiments built to identify a compression mechanism measure something else. The $L{=}1$ rung is itself a single coherent rule and sits above the null at every size ($55.6 \pm 0.7$ at 86M, $62.6 \pm 2.2$ at 3.5M; every per-seed interval above $0.5$, one false string, lengths matched), so the absence of filtering for coherent rules is a property of the coherent conditions of Sections~\ref{sec:central}, \ref{sec:learnable}, \ref{sec:wikipedia}, \ref{sec:robustness} and \ref{sec:qwen}, not of every single-rule corruption. The ladder rule corrupts only the first multiplication and leaves the rest of the derivation correct, and we have no measured account beyond that.

\subsection{The Learnable-Selector Control: Predictability, Isolated}
\label{sec:learnable}

Both withdrawn designs move the metric by changing how many false strings a problem has. This control instead fixes the false answer to $a{\cdot}b + T[\,s(p)\bmod 10\,]$ for one 10-entry offset table $T$, so that \texttt{learnable}, \texttt{k2} and \texttt{k3} share one ten-string family, and varies only the selector $s(p)$. In \texttt{learnable} it is one visible operand's ones digit, a lookup the model can fit. In \texttt{k2} and \texttt{k3} it is the sum of two or three ones digits mod $10$, learnable in principle and progressively harder to fit. In \texttt{learnable\_mod} the index is the sum of all visible operands modulo the problem's family size, bypassing $T$; it is deterministic but not learned at any size. These sit alongside \texttt{random} (an unpredictable draw over a support of the same order) and \texttt{coherent} (one rule), all scored with the family measure on the length-matched subset of the held-out scale grid (single pass, no problem seen twice, null $0.500$ up to a ${\leq}1.3$-point correction; Table~\ref{tab:scale_grid}, Appendix~\ref{app:scale}), with a clean-corpus calibration at every size; realized shares at every size are in Table~\ref{tab:learnable_full}.

With mode count held fixed, the sign of the preference tracks learnability (Table~\ref{tab:learnable}). Against a selector it cannot learn (\texttt{random}, \texttt{learnable\_mod}) the correct completion holds two to seven points above the null at every size; against one it can (\texttt{learnable}) the model prefers the \emph{false} system, and the correct share falls to $43.4\% \pm 0.6\%$ at 474M (4 seeds). The intermediate selectors sit between the two ends: as $s$ depends on more digits the model fits it less, the realized false string carries less mass ($65 \to 89 \to 92$ at 474M) and the family share rises from below the null to the null ($43 \to 49 \to 49$ at 474M; $45 \to 49 \to 48$ at 418M); the unlearnable selectors sit above it.

A below-null cell can otherwise be a competence artifact, since a model that cannot yet do the arithmetic also scores under the null (Appendix~\ref{app:scale}). That reading does not apply here: \texttt{random} and \texttt{learnable} train on identical correct derivations and differ only in the false selector, so at matched correct-side competence the selector is the only variable separating a cell above the null from one below it. The measured reading is that predictability, not description length, moves the preference: the ladder varied the number of constants a deterministic rule stores, with the table increasingly unlearned at higher $L$ (Section~\ref{sec:ladder}), and the family share did not rise; here the stored table is fixed, the learnability of its index varies, and the preference crosses the null. One caution remains: \texttt{learnable\_mod} also changes the size of the false family (the full random family of ${\approx}17.5$ strings instead of the 10-entry table), so it anchors the unlearnable end as a direction rather than a matched-diversity point; the clean matched-diversity gradient is \texttt{learnable}$\to$\texttt{k2}$\to$\texttt{k3}. The contrast holds at 1.12B, 2.16B and 3.08B parameters (2 seeds per cell): \texttt{random} above the null, \texttt{coherent} at it and \texttt{learnable} below it, with the clean calibration above $96\%$, so the measure is not manufacturing the gaps.

\subsection{Transfer to Wikipedia Text with Entity Substitution}
\label{sec:wikipedia}

In pair accuracy the two conditions again separate (Table~\ref{tab:wikipedia}, Appendix~\ref{app:transfer}): $69.5$--$71.5\%$ under random substitution and $45.9$--$48.7\%$ under coherent substitution across 3.5M--86M (4 seeds). Only the coherent column is interpretable: one global mapping yields one false paragraph per prompt, its null is $50\%$, and a consistent substitution is not filtered on real text either. Random substitution replaces every entity site (a median of eight per paragraph), its support cannot be enumerated, and on held-out paragraphs a frequency-matching model wins nearly every comparison, so that column's null is close to $100\%$ and we draw no conclusion from it or from its per-entity-type breakdown (Table~\ref{tab:wiki_entity}). The false paragraph is longer by $28.1$ characters under random and $27.8$ under coherent substitution, so length does not explain the difference between the conditions, but only $1\%$ of pairs are length-matched and neither column has the length control of Section~\ref{sec:central}.

The enumerable special case, single-entity paragraphs, quantifies why natural text resists the measure (Table~\ref{tab:wiki_single}, Appendix~\ref{app:transfer}). Its random support is one entity type's pool of ${\sim}2800$ strings: a clean 86M model with no false rule reads $5.6\%$ of the mass on the correct string, the random-trained model $4.0\%$, both leak-dominated. On the one-mode coherent support the length-matched subset, pooled over 8 runs ($n{=}25$ per cell), reads $52.4 \pm 4.0$ at the $50$ null, consistent with the paired result but underpowered per cell.

\subsection{Robustness Checks}
\label{sec:robustness}

\textbf{Standard corpus.}\quad In the standard setup each problem appears once, correct or incorrect, and problems repeat often enough that frequencies are exactly known. The aggregate share reads $50.2 \pm 0.7$, $57.6 \pm 0.2$, $59.0 \pm 0.3$ and $59.4 \pm 0.6$ for random errors and $47.3 \pm 0.7$, $49.2 \pm 0.4$, $50.0 \pm 0.4$ and $50.1 \pm 0.1$ for coherent ones (4 seeds; $5.51$ false modes on average). Here the contrast appears from 12M, two sizes earlier than on the denoising corpus, and is absent at 3.5M, where pair accuracy on the same support nevertheless reads $76.3\% \pm 1.5\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:standard_share}; the standard-versus-denoising pair-accuracy comparison is Table~\ref{tab:standard_vs_denoising}).

\textbf{Noise ratio on the controlled measure.}\quad With $K$ random errors per problem the frequency-matching null is exactly $1/(K{+}1)$. At 86M (2 seeds, non-strict support of 4565 problems with a mean of $8.2$ false modes) the family share reads $56.3 \pm 0.7$, $44.9 \pm 0.2$ and $36.1 \pm 0.7$ at $K{=}1, 2, 4$ against nulls of $50.0$, $33.3$ and $20.0$. The absolute share falls as pair accuracy on the same support does ($88.7 \to 79.4 \to 76.3$; the original pair-accuracy-only sweep is Table~\ref{tab:noise}), but the excess above the null does not shrink: $+6.3$, $+11.6$, $+16.1$ on the full set and $+7.3$, $+7.6$, $+11.4$ on the length-matched subset (Table~\ref{tab:kratio}, Appendix~\ref{app:standard}). Its $K{=}1$ value is below the strict $59.0$ because the non-strict support keeps problems whose strings collide across problems. We read this as the preference not shrinking with noise, not as strengthening.

\textbf{Tokenization.}\quad Under a BPE tokenizer (SentencePiece, vocabulary 1000) the coherent condition stays at chance in pair accuracy, and its denoising value moves from $43.5\%$ at character level to $49.3\%$, consistent with the length account; the random column rises too, but on a multi-mode support that rise is not interpretable (pair accuracy only; Table~\ref{tab:bpe}).

\textbf{Frequency.}\quad For coherent errors frequency dominates: at 40/60 the model prefers the false system in $72\%$ of pairs and at 20/80 in $90\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:frequency}), numbers that one false string leaves unaffected by splitting. For random errors the minority regime has only pair accuracy, and the aggregate share at 50/50 in that setup is already at chance ($50.2 \pm 0.7$), so we claim no persistence of a random-error preference there.

\textbf{Matched control.}\quad ``Matched random'' errors that corrupt as many steps as the coherent rule give $74.5\% \pm 0.4\%$ pair accuracy against $47.2\%$ for coherent, which rules out corruption load but not mode count (Appendix~\ref{app:standard}); a chained-verification variant that raises coherent pair accuracy is reported, unmeasured on the share, in Appendix~\ref{app:chained}.

\subsection{Architecture and Scale: Qwen3 at 418M and 490M}
\label{sec:qwen}

Two checks address whether the contrast is tied to the GPT-2-like design or to toy scale. A Qwen3-0.6B configuration \citep{yang2025qwen3} (RoPE, GQA, SwiGLU, RMSNorm; $417.6$M parameters with our character vocabulary, labeled 418M throughout) trained from scratch on 50/50 corpora of the standard construction scaled to 1.4M problems (35k steps, batch 16, sequence length 256, bf16; 3 seeds) gives $87.3\% \pm 0.4\%$ pair accuracy under random errors and $49.4\% \pm 1.3\%$ under coherent ones. On the same checkpoints the aggregate share puts the correct completion at $56.3\% \pm 0.3\%$ against the enumerated support for random errors and $49.7\% \pm 0.0\%$ for coherent ones (null $50\%$). The contrast holds on the controlled measure at 418M as at 86M, and a coherent rule is not filtered on either metric.

A Qwen3-architecture model of 490M parameters (labeled ${\sim}1$B in an earlier draft; it instantiates to $489.9$M at the run's BPE-8000 vocabulary) was trained from scratch on 1~GB of FineWeb-Edu \citep{penedo2024fineweb} with mathematical content filtered out and replaced by 8\% contradictory math (15{,}000 steps, batch 4, sequence length 512, lr $3\text{e-}4$ with 200 warmup steps, fp16, ${\approx}31$M tokens). It gives $77.6\% \pm 0.8\%$ pair accuracy under random errors and $47.4\% \pm 0.7\%$ under coherent ones (2 seeds, the first on a Kaggle T4 and the second on an H100). This is pair accuracy on an enumeration-free support, so the random value carries no quantitative claim; what the run establishes is that a coherent false rule is not filtered at 490M on real text with the contradictory material in the minority. The replication of the controlled contrast at 1.12B, 2.16B and 3.08B is in Table~\ref{tab:learnable}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%======================================================================

\textbf{Summary of findings.}\quad Two math corpora agree at 86M: a false system whose output the model cannot predict from the problem loses about nine points of probability mass to the correct one ($59.0\%$ denoising, $59.4\%$ standard), and a coherent false rule loses none in any coherent condition we built, on every corpus and at every size we tested (the one exception is the ladder's first rung, a single-rule corruption of a different construction; Section~\ref{sec:ladder}). Below 86M the corpora disagree, so the effect is established at one size and nowhere below, while generation, which no splitting argument touches, separates the conditions at every size. Section~\ref{sec:learnable} isolates the axis: over enumerated false families of the same order (a mean of $17.5$ strings for the unpredictable selector, ten for the learnable ones), whether the model can learn which member applies moves the preference from ${+}5$ points for the correct system to ${-}7$ against it; the partly learnable selectors that share the learnable one's ten-string table sit at the null.

\textbf{What we withdraw.}\quad Preference is not graded in the description length of the false system. The two experiments built to show it move the pairwise readings by 32 and 16 points (pair accuracy in one, the realized share in the other) while leaving the family share flat or falling; their apparent signal is the model's inability to predict which false string will appear, credited to the correct side by a pairwise comparison. Coherent errors do not fall below chance (that was completion length), and the effect is not present at every scale. None of these three claims from the first version survives its own control. Pair accuracy remains a legitimate measure of ranking behavior, but any design that varies the number of false forms (competing rules, noise ratios, entity substitution, corruption load) varies the metric along with the treatment.

\textbf{The role of frequency.}\quad Coherent falsehood needs no frequency advantage to neutralize any preference for the correct answer (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}); for random errors the excess above the frequency-matching null persists to four errors per problem at 86M, and on the standard corpus, where only pair accuracy exists, we make no minority-regime claim.

\textbf{Implications and scope.}\quad Within these settings the training objective offers no inherent ``truth compass''. In every coherent condition we built, internally coherent falsehood stays competitive at every size, on synthetic math and on diluted natural text; the preference against incoherent falsehood is real but modest where we can measure it cleanly; and a false system the model \emph{can} predict is preferred over the correct one (Section~\ref{sec:learnable}). What the objective favors is therefore a learnable, internally consistent system rather than a true one. That bears on data curation, where coordinated errors would evade diversity-based filtering and where a corruption with a learnable rule is the kind our setting favored, and on the reading of hallucination as a corpus-statistics phenomenon \citep{kalai2024calibrated}. Our models remain far below production scale, compression remains an interpretation rather than a measured quantity, and we make no claims about production models.

%======================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%======================================================================

Measured against everything an error generator can produce rather than against one sampled error, correctness preference in contradictory training data is smaller than the pairwise metric reports and appears later: at 86M the correct completion holds $59.0\%$ of the probability mass where the pairwise comparison reports $77.6\%$, and below 86M the aggregate share shows no preference on the denoising corpus while pair accuracy reads $72$--$89\%$. A single coherent false rule is not filtered in any of our coherent conditions, at any size, architecture or corpus we built them on; the one single-rule corruption outside those conditions, the ladder's first rung, sits above the null. The preference does not scale with the description length of the false system; it moves with predictability, and making the selector of an enumerated false family learnable turns the correct system's small advantage into a preference for the false one, from 418M to 3.08B parameters. The behavioral finding is a preference for a learnable, internally consistent system over a true one, and we report it as such.

%======================================================================
\section*{Limitations}
%======================================================================

\textbf{Measurement.}\quad The aggregate share is exact only where the false support can be enumerated, which rules out natural-text settings in general, and it is a lower bound rather than a point estimate. It carries a downward bias that grows as the model shrinks (clean-corpus family share $99.7\%$ at 86M, $64.1\%$ at 3.5M; Section~\ref{sec:ladder}) and that depends on architecture at fixed size, a 413M GPT-2 leaking like a 202M one (Appendix~\ref{app:scale}). Where we report an effect the measure is close to unbiased; where we report none, a small preference could be hidden by the leak.

\textbf{Scale, in both directions.}\quad Under the aggregate share the effect is present only at the largest primary size on the denoising corpus and from 12M on the standard corpus, so there is one size at which it is established on both; on the held-out grid the random cells clear the null only from 418M, because the leak dominates below. The billion-scale replication (1.12B--3.08B, 2 seeds, single pass) exists; what remains open is compute-matched training at that scale and controlled evidence below 86M, where only generation separates the conditions at every size. A same-size 413M GPT-2 reproduces the above-null random cell only marginally ($51.2 \pm 1.1$ against a clean calibration of $93.8$), so an architecture component in the effect cannot be excluded (Appendix~\ref{app:scale}).

\textbf{Domain.}\quad Wikipedia entity substitution has no enumerable false support in general, and the single-entity special case (Section~\ref{sec:wikipedia}, Table~\ref{tab:wiki_single}) quantifies why rather than removing the obstacle: a ${\sim}2800$-string random support leaks $94$--$98$ points at 3.5M--86M, and the coherent support's length-matched subset holds only $n{=}25$ problems per cell. What transfers cleanly is the coherent result. Domains with competing real-world knowledge systems remain open.

\textbf{Discriminative versus generative.}\quad Pair accuracy ($87.4\%$) exceeds generative accuracy ($52.8\%$) at 86M, the familiar teacher-forcing gap, whose roots in next-token training are analyzed by \citet{bachmann2024pitfalls}. The generative result stands on its own.

\textbf{Causal identification.}\quad The learnable-selector control varies whether the selector over an enumerated false family is learnable, and three limits remain. It isolates predictability, not description length: the reading that the model prefers the \emph{more compressible} of two systems is consistent with the swing but not measured by it, since a learnable selector is both more predictable and cheaper to store. The ladder does not hold learnedness fixed, so the two are separated only in the direction the learnable control tests. The false family the learnable conditions can produce is ten strings per problem, not one, so the design separates predictability from mode \emph{count} but not from the existence of a small multiplicity. Below-null readings rest on a matched-competence argument rather than on a null that is clean below $0.5$, and all of it is one formal-math corpus family. Probing for ``truth'' against ``coherence'' directions in models trained under these conditions is a natural next step.

\textbf{Ethical considerations.}\quad Internally consistent misinformation can be harder for models to filter than diverse errors; this is a controlled diagnostic finding, not evidence of production-LLM vulnerability, and transparent reporting of such failure modes can inform defensive data curation. \section{The MDL Framing, and What It Predicted}
\label{app:mdl}

This appendix records the reasoning that motivated the experimental design, and what happened to it. All of it describes an idealized two-part MDL learner \citep{rissanen1978modeling,grunwald2007minimum}, not a theorem about gradient-descent training.

\textbf{Setup.}\quad A corpus $D$ of $n$ problems, each appearing with an answer from a correct rule system $T_1$ and one from an alternative $T_2$. The model must encode \emph{both} clusters, which we model as a latent-source mixture with per-problem assignments $z_i \in \{1,2\}$:
\[
L(M) + L(D|M) = \underbrace{L(T_1) + L(T_2)}_{\text{theories}} + \underbrace{L(\{z_i\})}_{\text{source assignments}} + \underbrace{\sum_i L(a_i | T_{z_i})}_{\text{answers given sources}}
\]
A learner that internalizes the more compressible theory encodes its answers cheaply and pays a higher per-token cost for the other cluster.

\textbf{Prediction 1} (random errors): if $T_1$ is compact and $T_2$ is a set of independent random answers, the correct cluster holds more probability mass. \emph{Partly borne out.} That quantity is the aggregate share of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. It exceeds $0.5$ at 86M ($59.0\% \pm 0.8\%$) and fails at 3.5M--26M on the denoising corpus ($18.6\%$, $31.6\%$, $44.1\%$), while the standard corpus shows it from 12M (Table~\ref{tab:standard_share}).

\textbf{Prediction 2} (coherent errors): if $L(T_1) \approx L(T_2)$, then at equal frequency the two clusters receive comparable mass. \emph{Borne out} in every coherent condition at every size, architecture and corpus we tested (Sections~\ref{sec:central}, \ref{sec:learnable}, \ref{sec:wikipedia}, \ref{sec:robustness}, \ref{sec:qwen}); the single-rule $L{=}1$ rung of the ladder, which sits above the null at every size, is the one measured exception (Section~\ref{sec:ladder}).

\textbf{Prediction 3} (multi-rule errors): $N$ compact rules with random assignment to problems cost an extra $O(n \log N)$ bits for the selector, which should restore the correct cluster's advantage. \emph{Not borne out.} Mass on the correct cluster reads $47.9$, $47.8$, $41.8$, $41.9$ and $38.7\%$ at $N = 1, 2, 3, 5, 10$ (Section~\ref{sec:multirule}), and the ladder of Section~\ref{sec:ladder} is flat at 86M ($55.6$, $51.9$, $52.6$, $51.3$).

The graded reading of this framework is not supported by our own measurement, and only its binary consequence (Prediction 2, and Prediction 1 at our largest size) survives. One prediction the graded reading did not make, but the learnability account does, is borne out: the extra cost the mixture pays for the false cluster is the source-assignment term $L(\{z_i\})$, paid only when $z_i$ cannot be recovered from the problem. Making it recoverable, as the learnable selector of Section~\ref{sec:learnable} does, should remove the cost and let the model commit to the false cluster, and the data are consistent with that: the correct share drops below the null there while the ladder, which left the assignment unpredictable at every rung, never moved. We report compression as an interpretation of the surviving contrast, not as a quantity these experiments measure.

\section{The Held-Out Scale Grid and the Learnable-Selector Control}
\label{app:scale}

This grid trains one model per condition on 500k problems for 5000 steps at batch 32 ($0.44$ epochs, so no problem is seen twice) and evaluates on ${\approx}2.5$k held-out problems disjoint from training by a hash of the problem parameters. It is the measurement behind Table~\ref{tab:learnable}. Because each problem is written once, correct with probability $0.5$ and otherwise with one false string, the frequency-matching null for the family share is exactly $0.500$ (a marginal correction from arithmetic problems that render identically in every mode raises it to at most $0.513$; we read the band $[0.500, 0.513]$, i.e.\ $50.0$--$51.3\%$). We report the length-matched subset, on which the completion-length confound of Section~\ref{sec:central} is removed.

\textbf{A null of indifference is also a null of competence.}\quad Every null here describes a model that both lacks a preference \emph{and} can compute the correct answer; a model that prefers truth but has not learned the arithmetic reads below it too. So a below-null cell is not on its own evidence of a preference for the false side. Only excursions above the band are unambiguous, except where a matched-competence contrast is available, as in the learnable control, where \texttt{random} and \texttt{learnable} share their correct-side training and so cannot differ in competence.

\textbf{The keyed condition.}\quad The held-out grid also contains a \emph{keyed} condition, in which the single false string per problem is selected by a hash of the problem parameters that the model cannot compute on an unseen problem. Scored against that realized string it reads $92.5 \pm 0.1$ at 418M and $92.7 \pm 0.3$ at 474M (2 seeds), but because the selector is unrecoverable the frequency-matching null for that realized measure is $\mathbb{E}[n/(n{+}1)] \approx 94.1$ over the per-problem family size $n$ (mean $17.5$), not $50$, so these values sit below their null. On the family support, where the null is $50$, keyed is the same measurement as \texttt{random}: the full-set family share is $49.1 \pm 0.8$ at 474M (2 seeds) against $49.6$ for \texttt{random}, and $13.3 \pm 1.3$, $24.6 \pm 1.5$, $30.4 \pm 0.6$ and $37.8 \pm 0.7$ at 3.5M--86M (4 seeds) against $12.7$, $24.1$, $30.0$ and $36.6$. The condition is recorded here for completeness and is not cited anywhere as a positive result.

\subsection{NLL Distribution}

Random errors produce a right-skewed per-pair NLL difference (mean $+0.048$, median $+0.025$, 81.5\% positive; one run per condition, seed 42, standard paired test set). Coherent errors produce a symmetric distribution (45.5\% positive). This is why pair accuracy and the mean per-pair NLL difference can diverge. The coherent distribution's center sits slightly below $0.5$ because the coherent false completions are shorter (by about $0.8$ tokens on this standard paired test set, $0.58$ on the denoising corpus); on length-matched problems the aggregate share reads $49.3$--$49.9\%$ on the standard corpus (Table~\ref{tab:standard_share}) and $46.7$--$49.3\%$ on the denoising grid (Section~\ref{sec:central}).

Wherever we computed both, the gap between pair accuracy and the aggregate share shrinks with model size ($53.1$, $52.5$, $45.1$ and $28.4$~pp at 3.5M, 12M, 26M and 86M on the denoising corpus), which alone would produce a declining pair-accuracy trend on a condition where only pair accuracy is available. We report the numbers and do not interpret the trend.

\subsection{Generation by Task Type}
\label{app:gentask}

For random-trained 86M models the generative accuracy of Table~\ref{tab:generative} splits by task as differentiation 79.5\%, factoring 70.4\%, equation solving 61.0\% and multi-step arithmetic 0\%; the last requires exact carry propagation across many digits, which autoregressive generation at this scale does not achieve even where paired evaluation discriminates well. Coherent-trained models are lower on every type that is generated at all, with the largest gap on factoring (70.4\% vs 38.3\%) and the smallest on equation solving (61.0\% vs 45.7\%); differentiation reads 79.5\% vs 56.5\%.

\subsection{Learning Curves}

All sizes reach a behavioral plateau by step 3000--4000. In the original large random-trained run (seed 42), pair accuracy on a 500-pair subsample of the standard paired test set reads 90.4\% at step 3000, 86.4\% at 4000 and 87.8\% at 5000. This check rules out an early-stopping artifact; the underlying logs and plotting script are in the released repository.

    \item[] Question: Do the main claims made in the abstract and introduction accurately reflect the paper's contributions and scope?
    \item[] Answer: \answerYes{}

The abstract and introduction state that the effect is established at 86M on two corpora and not below, that pair-accuracy results elsewhere in the paper are uncontrolled and overstate the excess above chance by a factor of three to four, and that the multi-rule and description-length experiments are reported as negative controls rather than as evidence for a mechanism. Claims that appeared in the first version of this work and did not survive their own controls are named and withdrawn in Sections~\ref{sec:multirule}, \ref{sec:ladder} and \ref{sec:discussion}; the learnable-selector control of Section~\ref{sec:learnable} is described as isolating predictability, with compression left as an interpretation.

    \item[] Guidelines:
    \begin{itemize}

        \item The authors are encouraged to create a separate ``Limitations'' section in their paper.
        \item The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings, model well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the implications would be.
        \item The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.
        \item The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach.
